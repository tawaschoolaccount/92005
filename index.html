Kaitiakitanga Advisory

Kaitiakitanga is a traditional Māori concept meaning guardianship, stewardship, and protection. Rooted in the Māori worldview of the interconnectedness of all things, it involves sustainably managing the environment and passing resources on to future generations in a healthy state.

About

The degradation of New Zealand's environment stems largely from the historical overhunting of mega-herbivores like the moa, which permanently disrupted native food webs. This loss of specialized plant-eating and seed-dispersing birds caused ecosystem-wide imbalances, triggering widespread vegetation changes and rendering ancient forest habitats highly vulnerable to collapse. Because New Zealand evolved in isolation, its native birds developed highly specialized ecological roles. When these species were overhunted (most notably the large flightless birds like the Moa, Haast's eagle, and adzebill), the ecosystem lost its primary grazers, seed dispersers, and apex predators. The environmental degradation manifested in several key ways:

Many native birds, such as the kererū and kōkako, are essential for regenerating native forests because they eat and disperse the seeds of large canopy trees. Removing these birds limits forest growth, directly impacting the habitats other species rely on

New Zealand birds evolved for millions of years without mammalian predators and lack natural defenses. Historically, unregulated hunting quickly decimated slow-breeding and flightless birds (like the moa and huia), forever altering the food web and removing key components of the natural ecosystem

In alpine environments, birds like the kea play vital roles as foragers and scavengers. Because native birds occupy specialized ecological niches, the removal of any single species causes an imbalance that can lead to vegetation die-offs or the unchecked spread of certain insects

Although primarily related to the hunting of introduced game animals (like deer and tahr), the use of lead ammunition continues to cause fatal poisoning in native alpine birds, particularly kea, which scavenge on remaining gut piles and carcasses

Even low-level or localized hunting of rare native birds forces the surviving populations into genetic bottlenecks, decreasing their ability to adapt to climate change, diseases, and invasive predators
